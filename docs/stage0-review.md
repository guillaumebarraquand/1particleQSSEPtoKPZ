## 1. The nine choices of `README.md`

Answers are asked on all nine. The numbers below are those of `README.md`. Choices 1, 4, 5 and 6 carry the mathematical content.

**Choice 1. Stochastic integrals.** The Itô integrals $\int_0^t\psi_s(x\pm1)\ dW_s$ in (1.7) are expressed as limits in probability of left-point Riemann sums along the uniform subdivisions of $[0,t]$. The Itô–Walsh integral is defined as the smallest relation that contains the elementary predictable fields, is stable under addition and is closed under $L^2$ limits.

**Choice 4. The solution of the stochastic heat equation (`IsMildSolution`).**

*What the article says* (Section 1.4, and Section 7.3). The solution is "the random field $Z(t,x)$, adapted to the filtration generated by the white noise", which satisfies (1.13) for all $t>0$ and $x\in\mathbb R$. Existence and uniqueness "in a suitable class of random fields" are quoted from the literature, and the continuous positive version is always taken.

*What Lean asks.* Given a filtration $(\mathcal G_t,\ t\ge0)$ and a white noise $\xi$ with respect to it, a random field $Z(t,x,\omega)$ satisfies `IsMildSolution` when:

* (a) almost surely, $(t,x)\mapsto Z(t,x)$ is continuous on $(0,\infty)\times\mathbb R$;
* (b) for every $t>0$ and every $x\in\mathbb R$, the field $h_{t,x}(s,y)=\mathbf 1_{0<s<t}\ g_{t-s}(x-y)\ Z(s,y)$ is Walsh-integrable, and almost surely (the null set may depend on $(t,x)$)

$$Z(t,x)=g_t(x)+\int_0^t\int_{\mathbb R}g_{t-s}(x-y)\ Z(s,y)\ \xi(ds\ dy).$$

*What (b) contains without saying it.* In the Lean definition, a Walsh-integrable field is an $L^2(\Omega\times\mathbb R_+\times\mathbb R)$ limit of elementary predictable fields. So (b) forces, for every $t>0$ and $x$,

$$\int_0^t\int_{\mathbb R}g_{t-s}(x-y)^2\ \mathbb E\big[Z(s,y)^2\big]\ dy\ ds<\infty,$$

and it forces $h_{t,x}$ to agree almost everywhere with a predictable field. Since $g_{t-s}(x-y)>0$, the field $Z$ itself agrees almost everywhere on $(0,t)\times\mathbb R\times\Omega$ with a predictable field. So adaptedness is imposed through the stochastic integral, though not as a separate condition. Also $Z(t,x)\in L^2$ and $\mathbb E[Z(t,x)]=g_t(x)$ (proved in `Checks.lean`).

*Differences with the article.*

* The filtration may be any filtration for which $\xi$ is a white noise. It need not be the one generated by $\xi$.
* Adaptedness is not stated separately (it is implicit, as just explained).
* Positivity is not asked; it is a theorem (Mueller). The measure $\mathcal Z(t)$ is defined in Lean with $\max(Z(t,x),0)$, because `ENNReal.ofReal` discards negative values. It equals $Z(t,x)\ dx$ only once $Z\ge0$ is known, and this is neither a hypothesis of `IsMildSolution` nor part of the conclusion of `main_theorem`. It is therefore a separate statement to be proved, **P** `IsMildSolution.nonneg` (Section 2).
* No moment bound is asked, whereas the "suitable class" of the literature usually comes with one. It is not needed, because uniqueness holds without it (below).
* The initial condition $\delta_0$ is carried by the term $g_t(x)$; there is no separate condition at $t=0$.

*Why this class is the right one.* Two facts together say that `IsMildSolution` describes exactly one field, up to indistinguishability: the solution of the article. Therefore "for every $Z$ satisfying `IsMildSolution`" in `main_theorem` means "for the solution $Z$".

* **Existence (E3).** The solution of Chen–Dalang (Theorem 2.4), in its continuous version, satisfies (a) and (b). This is quoted, not proved in Lean (decision of 11 November 2026).
* **Uniqueness (U3).** Two fields $Z$, $Z'$ that satisfy (a) and (b) with the same noise are indistinguishable. This argument is not in the article, which refers to the literature. Let $D=Z-Z'$ and $F(t,x)=\mathbb E[D(t,x)^2]$.
  * $F(t,x)<\infty$ for every $t>0$ and $x$, since $Z(t,x)-g_t(x)$ and $Z'(t,x)-g_t(x)$ are Walsh integrals, hence in $L^2$.
  * By linearity of the Walsh integral and Itô's isometry, $F$ satisfies the equation displayed below, in which $K(t,x)=g_t(x)^2=(4\pi t)^{-1/2}\ g_{t/2}(x)$.
  * Iterating (by Tonelli's theorem, since all functions are nonnegative) gives $F=K^{\star k}\star F$ for every $k\ge1$. Here $\star$ is the convolution in time and space on $(0,\infty)\times\mathbb R$, and $K^{\star k}$ is computed with Beta integrals; see below.

$$F(t,x)=\int_0^t\int_{\mathbb R}K(t-s,x-y)\ F(s,y)\ dy\ ds,\qquad K^{\star k}(t,x)=\frac{t^{k/2-1}}{2^k\ \Gamma(k/2)}\ g_{t/2}(x).$$

So, for $0<t\le T$, $K^{\star k}(t,x)\le\varepsilon_k(T)\ K(t,x)$, with

$$\varepsilon_k(T)=\frac{2\sqrt\pi\ T^{(k-1)/2}}{2^k\ \Gamma(k/2)}\longrightarrow0\quad(k\to\infty).$$

Therefore, for $0<t\le T$,

$$F(t,x)=(K^{\star k}\star F)(t,x)\le\varepsilon_k(T)\ (K\star F)(t,x)=\varepsilon_k(T)\ F(t,x).$$

Since $F(t,x)$ is finite, letting $k\to\infty$ gives $F(t,x)=0$. Note that no bound on $F$ uniform in $(t,x)$ is needed: pointwise finiteness suffices, and it comes from (b). Hence $Z(t,x)=Z'(t,x)$ almost surely for each $(t,x)$. Almost surely, this holds simultaneously at all rational points, and by the continuity (a) it holds at all points of $(0,\infty)\times\mathbb R$.

*What would go wrong otherwise.* If the class were larger (for instance without the requirement that the integrand in (b) be Walsh-integrable), uniqueness could fail and `main_theorem` would be false. If it were empty, `main_theorem` would be true for an empty reason; E3 excludes this, but is not proved in Lean.

**Choice 5. "For every realization".** The theorem quantifies over all probability spaces carrying the QSSEP, and over all probability spaces carrying a solution of the stochastic heat equation. This is legitimate because the law of each object does not depend on the probability space: for the QSSEP by pathwise uniqueness (U1), for the stochastic heat equation by the uniqueness just described (U3).

**Choice 6. Versions.**

*The issue.* In Lean, for each $\omega$ and each $t$, $\mathcal Z_n(t)$ and $\mathcal Z(t)$ are defined directly by the formulas (1.15) and $\mathcal Z(t)(dx)=Z(t,x)^+dx$ ($\mathcal Z(0)=\delta_0$), as measures on $\mathbb R$. Nothing guarantees, from the definitions alone, that these measures are finite, that they depend continuously on $t$, or that $\omega\mapsto(t\mapsto\mathcal Z_n(t))$ is measurable. Convergence in distribution in $C([0,T],\mathcal M_+(\mathbb R))$ only makes sense for random variables with values in that space.

*What the conclusion of `main_theorem` says.* There exist random variables $\widetilde{\mathcal Z_n}$ (on the probability space of the QSSEP) and $\widetilde{\mathcal Z}$ (on that of the white noise), with values in $C([0,T],\mathcal M_+(\mathbb R))$, such that:

* almost surely, for **all** $t\in[0,T]$, $\widetilde{\mathcal Z_n}(t)=\mathcal Z_n(t)$; and almost surely, for all $t\in[0,T]$, $\widetilde{\mathcal Z}(t)=\mathcal Z(t)$;
* $\widetilde{\mathcal Z_n}$ converges in distribution to $\widetilde{\mathcal Z}$ in $C([0,T],\mathcal M_+(\mathbb R))$.

The order "almost surely, for all $t$" makes $\widetilde{\mathcal Z_n}$ indistinguishable from $\mathcal Z_n$, not merely a modification. Two random variables with this property are almost surely equal, so the law of $\widetilde{\mathcal Z}$ and of $\widetilde{\mathcal Z_n}$ is uniquely determined, and the statement does not depend on which versions are chosen.

*What this contains.* Almost surely:

* for the QSSEP, $\mathcal Z_n(t)$ has finite mass for all $t\in[0,T]$, and $t\mapsto\mathcal Z_n(t)$ is weakly continuous. For $L=\infty$, the finiteness is $\sum_{y\in\mathbb Z}e^{n^{-1/4}y}\ P(nt/2,y)<\infty$, which comes from the bound (2.2);
* for the stochastic heat equation, $\int_{\mathbb R}Z(t,x)^+dx<\infty$ for all $t\in(0,T]$, $t\mapsto Z(t,x)^+dx$ is weakly continuous on $(0,T]$, and $Z(t,x)^+dx\to\delta_0$ weakly as $t\downarrow0$.

In the article these facts are implicit in the phrase "convergence in distribution in the space $C([0,T],\mathcal M_+)$". In Lean they have to be proved. `PLAN.md` (Section 3, row "End of Section 7") proves those concerning $\mathcal Z$ by deducing them from the limit, so that the continuity of the solution at $t=0$ is not needed as an input.

**Choices 2, 3, 7, 8, 9** concern the encoding of the white noise, the use of the equation (1.7) for every $L$ instead of $U_te_0$, almost sure hypotheses without usual conditions, the sequence $n_k$, and negative times. See `README.md`.

## 2. The companion statements (`QSSEPtoKPZ/Companions.lean`)

They say that the hypotheses of `main_theorem` can be met, that they determine the objects, and (P) that the measure $\mathcal Z(t)$ of the statement is $Z(t,x)\ dx$. Notation: $(\Omega,\mathcal F,(\mathcal F_t,\ t\ge0),\mathbb P)$ is a filtered probability space. "Brownian family" means `IsBrownianFamily`, i.e. independent standard $(\mathcal F_t)$-Brownian motions $B^{(x,j)}$, $x\in\mathbb Z$, $j\in\lbrace0,1\rbrace$. $W^{(x)}=(B^{(x,0)}+iB^{(x,1)})/\sqrt2$. "White noise" means `IsWhiteNoise`, with $\xi$ the white noise whose distribution function is $S$.

**E1** `exists_isWaveFunction` (Proposition 2.1 for $L=\infty$, Section 1.3 for $L<\infty$). For every $L\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}\cup\lbrace\infty\rbrace$ there exist a filtered probability space, a Brownian family on it, and a process $\psi$ satisfying `IsWaveFunction L`, i.e. solving (1.7) on $\Lambda_L$ from $\psi_0=e_0$.

*Remark.* This is weak existence (on some probability space). The stronger statement, "on **every** filtered probability space carrying a Brownian family there is such a $\psi$" (strong existence, as in Proposition 2.1), is also true and is what Package L will construct, but it is longer to prove without the usual conditions on the filtration. Weak existence is what is needed to show that `main_theorem` is not vacuous. To be decided by the author.

**U1** `isWaveFunction_unique`. If $\psi$ and $\psi'$ both satisfy `IsWaveFunction L` with the same filtration and the same Brownian family, then almost surely $\psi_t=\psi'_t$ for all $t\ge0$.

**E2** `exists_isWhiteNoise`. There exist a filtered probability space and a field $S$ satisfying `IsWhiteNoise`.

**W** (Section 7.2), in two parts.

* `IsWalshIntegral.ae_eq`: if $X=\int h\ d\xi$, $X'=\int h'\ d\xi$, and $h=h'$ almost everywhere on $\Omega\times\mathbb R\times\mathbb R$ (for $\mathbb P\otimes ds\otimes dy$), then $X=X'$ almost surely.
* `IsWalshIntegral.isometry`: if $X=\int h\ d\xi$, then $h\in L^2(\Omega\times\mathbb R\times\mathbb R)$, $X\in L^2(\Omega)$, and $\mathbb E[X^2]=\mathbb E\iint h(s,y)^2\ ds\ dy$.

*Remark.* The square integrability of $h$ and of $X$ is stated explicitly (the read-back pointed out that $X\in L^2$ was missing in the first draft), so that the identity cannot hold for the degenerate reason that Lean's integral of a non-integrable function is $0$.

**U3** `isMildSolution_unique` (Section 7.3; the argument is in Choice 4 above). If $Z$ and $Z'$ both satisfy `IsMildSolution` with the same filtration and the same white noise, then almost surely $Z(t,x)=Z'(t,x)$ for all $t>0$ and $x\in\mathbb R$.

**P** `IsMildSolution.nonneg` (added on 12 November 2026, after a remark of the author). If $Z$ satisfies `IsMildSolution`, then almost surely $Z(t,x)\ge0$ for all $t>0$ and $x\in\mathbb R$.

*Why it is needed.* In Lean, $\mathcal Z(t)$ is `sheMeasure`, defined for $t\ne0$ as $\max(Z(t,x),0)\ dx$. Two lemmas of `Checks.lean`, proved completely, make this explicit: `integral_sheMeasure` says $\int\varphi\ d\mathcal Z(t)=\int\max(Z(t,x),0)\ \varphi(x)\ dx$, and `integral_sheMeasure_of_nonneg` says that this is $\int Z(t,x)\ \varphi(x)\ dx$ when $Z(t,\cdot)\ge0$. The corollary `IsMildSolution.integral_sheMeasure` (in `Corollaries.lean`, depending on the `sorry` of P) combines them: almost surely, for all $t>0$ and all $\varphi$, $\int\varphi\ d\mathcal Z(t)=\int Z(t,x)\ \varphi(x)\ dx$, which is the measure of the article.

*How it will be proved.* Not as an input: `PLAN.md` (Section 3, row "End of Section 7") deduces it from the limit, since $Z$ has the law of the density of a limit of the nonnegative measures $\mathcal Z_n$. This does not use E3. Strict positivity (Mueller) is not stated; it is not needed.

**E3** (existence of a field satisfying `IsMildSolution`) is not stated, by decision of 11 November 2026; a comment in `Companions.lean` says so.

## 3. The read-back: what to compare with the article

`docs/readback-2026-11-12.md` was written by an agent that saw only the Lean code (comments removed) and Mathlib, never the article or `README.md`. Its Section 3 lists 14 points. Those that only the author can settle, by comparison with the article:

**Point 1, equation (1.7).** As read from Lean: for $x\in\Lambda_L$,

$$\psi_t(x)=\mathbf 1_{x=0}-\frac{D(x,x)}{2}\int_0^t\psi_s(x)\ ds+i\int_0^t\psi_s(x+1)\ dW_s^{(x)}+i\int_0^t\psi_s(x-1)\ d\overline{W_s^{(x-1)}},$$

with $W^{(x)}=(B^{(x,0)}+iB^{(x,1)})/\sqrt2$ independent, $\psi\equiv0$ outside $\Lambda_L$, and $D(x,x)=1$ at $x=\pm L$. Check the factor $\tfrac12$, the $\sqrt2$, the factors $i$, and which Brownian motion and which conjugate go with which neighbour.

**Point 10, equations (1.14)–(1.15).** As read from Lean: $\mathcal Z_n(t)$ has atoms at the points $(y-n^{3/4}t)/n^{1/2}$, $y\in\Lambda_L$, with masses

$$\exp\Big(n^{-1/4}y-\frac{\sqrt n\ t}{2}-\frac t{24}\Big)\ \big|\psi_{nt/2}(y)\big|^2 .$$

Check the time $nt/2$, the centring $n^{3/4}t$, the scale $n^{1/2}$, the tilt and the two corrections.

**Point 7, equation (1.13).** $g_t(x)=e^{-x^2/(2t)}/\sqrt{2\pi t}$, i.e. $\partial_tZ=\tfrac12\partial_x^2Z+Z\xi$, with $\xi$ of intensity $1$ and $Z(0,\cdot)=\delta_0$.

**Point 8.** `IsMildSolution` asks neither positivity nor adaptedness of $Z$, and $\mathcal Z(t)$ is $\max(Z(t,x),0)\ dx$ for $t>0$ and $\delta_0$ at $t=0$ by definition. See Choices 4 and 6 above, and the statement P of Section 2, added for this reason.

**Points 2, 4, 5, 13** (the encodings of the Itô integral, of the Brownian family and of the white noise). These are the choices 1, 2 and 7 of `README.md`, and the read-back describes them correctly. Its remark that `IsWhiteNoise` only states independence for adjacent space intervals within one time slab is right. Independence for disjoint rectangles follows from it and from the independence of the past: a subfamily of an independent family is independent, and different time slabs are separated with `indep_past`.

**Point 9.** No existence statement for `IsMildSolution` (E3): this is the decision of 11 November 2026.

Point 6 led to a change: `IsWalshIntegral.isometry` now also concludes $X\in L^2(\nu)$. The other points (3, 11, 12, 14) agree with `README.md` and call for no action.
